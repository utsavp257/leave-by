# leave-by — design

How long it actually takes to get to a New York airport, which way you should
go, and what the worst case costs you.

## Thesis

Everyone who flies out of New York has made the same guess: leave three hours
early, take a car, hope. Nobody knows whether that's right from their own
address, and the advice you find online is assertion rather than measurement —
travel guides say the AirTrain is "more predictable" and quote ranges like
"70–90 minutes" without ever showing a distribution.

The useful insight is that **a trip to the airport is the one journey where you
do not care about the average.** Miss the median and you're mildly late for
something. Miss a flight and the day is gone. So the quantity that matters is
the tail, and once you optimise the tail rather than the middle, the advice can
invert: a mode that is slower on a typical day can be the correct choice if its
worst case is tighter and cheaper.

Nobody has checked whether that inversion actually happens. That is the piece.

## Prior art, and the honest position

**Todd Schneider, "Analyzing 1.1 Billion NYC Taxi and Uber Trips, with a
Vengeance" (2015).** He answered the first half of this: travel-time
distributions from each neighbourhood to each airport by hour of day, using
weekday taxi trips through June 2015, with percentile framing. His numbers —
median Midtown to JFK of 64 minutes between 4 and 5pm, 38 minutes between 10 and
11am with a 90% chance of under 50 minutes — are the reference point.

He is the most-read analyst of NYC open data and this project must cite him
plainly and up front. Anything that reads as though it were discovered fresh
will be recognised and discounted.

Two things make this a new piece rather than a refresh:

**The mode question is unowned.** Todd measured taxis only. Nobody has put car
travel-time distributions beside rail travel-time distributions for the same
origins, which is the comparison a traveller actually faces. It also cannot be
done from public aggregates — it needs reconstructed per-trip subway
observations, which is exactly what stringline already holds.

**The city he measured no longer exists.** Since June 2015:

- Ride-hail went from marginal to dominant. The monthly high-volume for-hire
  file is 510 MB against yellow cab's 65 MB; the mode most people now use is
  not the mode he measured.
- Congestion pricing began 5 January 2025, adding a $1.50 per-trip charge on
  high-volume for-hire vehicles travelling to, from, within or through the zone.
- The Port Authority added a $3.50 ride-hail fee at JFK, LGA and EWR effective
  15 March 2026 — five months old and unmeasured.

So the framing is: *Todd Schneider answered half of this eleven years ago. Here
is the half he didn't, in a city that has changed underneath it.*

## Scope: three airports, three strengths of evidence

The three airports do not have equally good data, and the piece is clearer if it
says so outright rather than implying a uniform analysis.

**JFK — full trip-level comparison.** Two rail routes reach it and both are
already in stringline's data: the A to Howard Beach-JFK Airport, and the E, J or
Z to Sutphin Blvd-Archer Av-JFK Airport, each connecting to the AirTrain. Car and
rail both have measured trip-level distributions, so the tail claim is fully
supported here. This is where the argument lives.

**LaGuardia — typical times and day-to-day variation, but no trip-level tail.**
The M60+ and Q70+ are both present in MTA's bus segment-speed data, hourly and by
day of week, continuously from January 2023 to June 2026. That gives segment
travel times good enough to build a journey time, and the subway leg feeding
either bus is already in stringline's data.

What it does **not** give is a trip-level distribution. The field is
`average_travel_time` — an average over the buses in that hour. There are many
rows per segment-hour (one per date), so the spread of those daily averages is
measurable, and that is a real component of what a traveller faces. But it is
**not** the same as the spread of individual trips, and it is narrower, because
averaging within an hour smooths the tail away. Critically, that error runs in
one direction: **it understates how unpredictable the bus is, which flatters the
bus.** Since the whole thesis is that tails should drive the decision, this must
be stated plainly wherever an LGA number appears, and LGA must not be presented
as though its evidence were as strong as JFK's.

**Newark — car only.** NJ Transit publishes monthly route-level on-time
percentages and GTFS schedules, but no trip-level historical archive comparable
to the subway or bus position data. A proportion of trains meeting a punctuality
standard cannot be turned into a journey-time distribution. So EWR gets car
distributions and an explicit note that its transit side is unmeasurable from
public data, rather than a fabricated comparison.

### The upgrade that would put LGA on equal footing

A raw archive of MTA bus vehicle positions exists at
`https://s3.amazonaws.com/nycbuspositions/YYYY/MM/YYYY-MM-DD-bus-positions.csv.xz`,
roughly 20–25 MB compressed per day. Verified: 200 for sampled days in 2018,
2021, 2023, 2024-10 and 2024-11-04; **404 from 2024-11-18 onward.** So it runs
from about July 2017 to early November 2024 and then stops.

This is the bus analogue of what subwaydata.nyc is for the subway — reconstructing
trips from positions would give genuine bus tails, and its window overlaps
stringline's autumn corpus for 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024. It is also a
substantial job, and it cannot speak to the congestion-pricing era at all.

**Not in scope for this piece.** It is the obvious follow-up if the LGA
limitation turns out to be the thing readers push on, and worth recording now
because a dead archive tends to stay dead — anyone wanting this data later still
has it, but nobody is adding to it.

## The three questions

**1. How long does it take, really?**

Car travel-time distributions to each of the three airports, by origin zone and
time block, from TLC trip records — the Todd update, using high-volume for-hire
trips rather than taxis, and covering the congestion-pricing period.

Trip duration comes from the recorded pickup and dropoff timestamps. Worth
noting for the ride-hail data: `request_datetime` precedes `pickup_datetime`, so
there is a waiting component available that a taxi hail does not have. Whether
to count it is a real choice — the honest answer is to show both, because "when
must I open the app" is the question a reader actually has, and it is not the
same as "how long is the ride".

**2. Should you take the train?**

The centrepiece. For JFK: rail distributions against car distributions, for the
same origins and the same time blocks, with the tail marked on both.

The claim to test is the one every travel guide asserts without evidence — that
rail is slower but tighter. If it holds, the reader's decision rule changes. If
it doesn't hold, that is equally worth publishing, and more surprising.

LaGuardia appears here too, but visibly weaker: subway plus M60+ or Q70+ against
the car, on typical times and day-to-day variation only. Mark it differently in
the chart — a distribution and a range are not the same object and should not
share an encoding. A reader who takes the LGA line as seriously as the JFK line
has been misled by the design, not by the text.

**3. What does the worst case cost you — in minutes and in dollars?**

This is where Uber Shuttle belongs, as the clearest illustration of the piece's
whole point: a mode can be much cheaper without being any safer.

The buffer: the gap between a typical trip and a trip you can rely on. Then the
money, because the two move together and it is a satisfying place to end.

A car now carries the fare, plus the $9 congestion toll or the $1.50 for-hire
surcharge where applicable, plus the new $3.50 Port Authority fee. Rail is a
subway fare plus the AirTrain. **Verify both fare figures before publishing** —
sources disagree, quoting around $10.50 for subway plus AirTrain, and the
AirTrain fare has risen recently.

If the slower mode turns out to be both cheaper and tighter in the tail, that is
the finding, and it needs no further embellishment.

## Method

### Distributions, not averages

Everything is a distribution summarised by percentiles. The headline quantity is
a **leave-by time**: the departure time at which the chosen percentile of the
travel-time distribution still gets you there by your deadline.

### The constraint that shapes the whole design

Stringline's `build_stats.py` already makes the correct methodological point, in
its own docstring:

> "waiting is a property of the station and the hour, not of the pair, and
> percentiles do not add: the 90th percentile of a wait plus the 90th percentile
> of a ride is not the 90th percentile of the two combined, it is worse than
> either."

That is exactly right and it constrains this project. A door-to-door rail tail
is a wait *plus* a ride *plus* an AirTrain leg, and those cannot be combined by
adding percentiles. They have to be convolved, which needs the underlying
histograms rather than the summary percentiles.

`build_stats.py` builds those histograms and then discards them, persisting only
`p50` and `p90` per pair per block, plus a single median wait per station per
block.

**So there is a prerequisite change in stringline**, and it is small:

- persist the ride histograms for the pairs that reach Howard Beach and Sutphin
  Blvd — a few hundred pairs, not all 1,653 on the A line alone
- persist wait *distributions*, not just the median, for those same origin
  stations
- while there, emit `p95` alongside `p50` and `p90`

Until that lands, the deepest percentile available is p90, so **early drafts
should be written on p90 and say so**, rather than claiming a 95% figure the
data cannot support.

### Alignment

Stringline's blocks are rider-shaped rather than clock-shaped: early (00:00–07:00),
am (07:00–10:00), midday (10:00–16:00), pm (16:00–19:00), evening (19:00–24:00).
TLC data is timestamped to the second, so aggregate the car side **up** to those
same five blocks. Matching the coarser source is the honest direction; presenting
hourly cars against block-level rail would invite a comparison the rail data
cannot support.

Periods also differ. Stringline covers 436 days sampled across five autumns,
2021-09-01 to 2025-12-31. TLC is continuous and current to 2026-05. **Restrict
the mode comparison to overlapping autumn months.** Use the full TLC range only
for the car-only sections, and say which figures rest on which window.

Stringline drops any pair with fewer than 30 samples; carry the same floor
through so both sides are filtered alike.

## Data

### Uber and Lyft: the `fhvhv` files are the Uber and Lyft data

Worth stating plainly, because the filename hides it. There is no separate Uber
or Lyft feed to find — **`fhvhv` is it.** TLC created the High-Volume For-Hire
Services licence category in August 2018 for bases dispatching more than 10,000
trips a day, and from 2019 those trips are reported in their own, more detailed
dataset. The company is identified by `hvfhs_license_num`:

- **`HV0003` — Uber**
- **`HV0005` — Lyft**
- `HV0002` Juno and `HV0004` Via appear in older files; both are defunct

Uber's own public travel-time product, **Uber Movement, has been discontinued.**
Do not spend time looking for it. TLC is the source, and it is better anyway,
because it is per-trip rather than zone-averaged.

**TLC trip records** — verified reachable 2026-08-17:

| file | status | size |
|---|---|---|
| `yellow_tripdata_2026-04.parquet` | 200 | 64.8 MB |
| `yellow_tripdata_2026-05.parquet` | 200 | 69.7 MB |
| `fhvhv_tripdata_2026-04.parquet` | 200 | 509.9 MB |

Base: `https://d37ci6vzurychx.cloudfront.net/trip-data/`

Airport zone IDs confirmed from `taxi_zone_lookup.csv`: **JFK 132, LaGuardia 138,
Newark 1.**

Verified `fhvhv` columns: `hvfhs_license_num`, `dispatching_base_num`,
`originating_base_num`, `request_datetime`, `on_scene_datetime`,
`pickup_datetime`, `dropoff_datetime`, `PULocationID`, `DOLocationID`,
`trip_miles`, `trip_time`, `base_passenger_fare`, `tolls`, `bcf`, `sales_tax`,
`congestion_surcharge`, `airport_fee`, `tips`, `driver_pay`,
`shared_request_flag`, `shared_match_flag`, `access_a_ride_flag`,
`wav_request_flag`, `wav_match_flag`, `cbd_congestion_fee`.

Note there are **two** congestion fields and they are different things:
`congestion_surcharge` is the older state surcharge, `cbd_congestion_fee` is the
January 2025 congestion-pricing charge. Do not conflate or double-count them.
`airport_fee` carries the Port Authority charge.

### Access: query the files where they sit

The ride-hail files are large but do not need downloading. DuckDB with `httpfs`
reads them remotely with column projection, so a filtered aggregate touches a
fraction of the file. **Verified working**: the query below returned in about six
seconds against the 510 MB April 2026 file.

```sql
INSTALL httpfs; LOAD httpfs;
SELECT hvfhs_license_num, count(*), median(trip_time), quantile_cont(trip_time, 0.9)
FROM read_parquet('https://d37ci6vzurychx.cloudfront.net/trip-data/fhvhv_tripdata_2026-04.parquet')
WHERE DOLocationID = 132
GROUP BY 1;
```

This removes the bulk-download step from the plan entirely and makes iterating on
the car side cheap.

### Baseline already measured

Trips **to JFK**, April 2026, recorded here so later runs have something to
reconcile against:

| | trips | median | p90 | avg fare | airport fee | cbd fee |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Uber (HV0003) | 296,205 | 40.1 min | 71.4 min | $70.19 | $3.48 | $0.47 |
| Lyft (HV0005) | 81,842 | 39.9 min | 68.3 min | $66.40 | $3.51 | $0.44 |

Three things fall out of this immediately, and they shape the piece:

**The tail is the story, and it is large.** A median of 40 minutes against a p90
of 71. The trip you plan for is forty minutes; one in ten takes over seventy. The
buffer is nearly as long as the trip. That is the whole thesis, visible in the
first query.

**Which app you use does not matter for time.** Uber and Lyft are within a couple
of minutes of each other at both the median and the tail, which is what you would
expect from two fleets on the same roads. They differ on price, not risk. This
kills any "which service is faster" angle, and that is useful to know before
building it.

**The averaged `cbd_congestion_fee` of $0.47 is not the $1.50 rate.** It is
diluted because most JFK trips never touch the zone. Any per-trip cost figure
must condition on trips that actually enter it, or it will be wrong.

Uber also carries 3.6× Lyft's volume here, and Lyft's shared matching has
effectively stopped — 1 matched trip against Uber's 2,758 in the month.

### Validated against Todd, and the result changes the headline

The pipeline is built and reconciles: weekday-only JFK arrivals give p50 41, p90
73, p95 84 minutes, against the 40/71 measured across all days. Weekdays being
slightly worse is what one would expect.

The spot-check against Todd's 2015 figures did **not** agree, so it was chased
down rather than accepted. Midtown Center (zone 161) to JFK, weekdays, April
2026, by pickup hour:

| hour | median | p90 | median miles |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 02 | 32 | 40 | 17.5 |
| 07 | 60 | 74 | 17.2 |
| 10 | 62 | 84 | 17.2 |
| 15 | 88 | 109 | 17.2 |
| 20 | 48 | 59 | 17.5 |

**It is not a bug.** Median distance is a constant 17.2 miles across every hour of
the day, so the zone and the routing are stable; only the time moves. The curve
itself is physically sensible — fastest at 2am, worst at 3pm.

And it is not a mode artefact either. Todd measured yellow cabs, so the same trip
was run through the yellow file for the same month: **59 minutes at 10am against
ride-hail's 62.** Two fleets on the same roads at the same hour, three minutes
apart. Which car you take does not matter.

So the divergence is real. **The Midtown-to-JFK trip that took 38 minutes at
10–11am in Todd's data takes about 60 today — roughly 58% longer, over the same
17 miles.** That is a stronger and more honest headline than the one this spec was
written around. It is not "here is Todd's analysis refreshed"; it is "the same
journey has got half again as long, and here is the receipt."

Two consequences for the plan:

- **Lead with the deterioration, not the mode question.** It is a bigger finding,
  it needs no counterintuitive twist to land, and it makes the citation of Todd
  load-bearing in a way that is flattering to both parties.
- **Drop any Uber-versus-Lyft or yellow-versus-ride-hail framing.** Checked, and
  there is nothing there. Recorded so nobody spends a day rediscovering it.

One caution before publishing this comparison: Todd's corpus ran 2009 to mid-2015,
so his quoted figure is an average over that window rather than a 2015 snapshot.
The honest phrasing is "compared with 2009–2015" and the change should not be
attributed to any single cause — ride-hail growth, delivery traffic and street
redesign all sit inside it.

### Uber Shuttle: a real option, no public data, and a conclusion anyway

**What it is.** Uber launched Shuttle in the New York metro in October 2024 with
Midtown-to-LaGuardia routes, expanded to JFK in March 2025 serving Terminals 4
and 5, and has since added Newark. Fourteen-passenger vehicles on fixed routes
with scheduled departures roughly every 30 minutes from about 5am to 10:45pm,
around $18 a seat at launch with a $10 promotional period. Manhattan stops at
Port Authority and Grand Central; Brooklyn stops at Chinatown and Atlantic
Terminal. **Verify current pricing, routes and hours before publishing** — this
product has changed repeatedly in under two years.

**It is not in the trip data, and it will not be.** `shared_match_flag` in the
`fhvhv` files marks UberPool-style matching inside ordinary for-hire trips, which
is a different product. A fourteen-seat vehicle running a published timetable is
licensed as a commuter van, and TLC licenses commuter vans separately from
for-hire vehicles. The published trip record datasets are yellow, green, FHV and
FHVHV only — there is no commuter van trip file. So there is no way to measure
Shuttle journey times from public data.

**The piece can still say something true about it, and it is the interesting
kind of conclusion.** The shuttle drives the same roads as an UberX. Its travel
time is drawn from the same traffic distribution measured above — median 40
minutes, p90 71 — so it inherits the entire tail. Fixed stops and a headway of up
to half an hour can only add to that.

So the shuttle is a **price** answer, not a **reliability** answer: far cheaper
than a $70 average fare, and no safer for catching a flight. That is worth stating
plainly, because "take the shuttle" circulates as general airport advice, and
under this piece's thesis it is good advice for your wallet and close to
irrelevant to your risk of missing the plane. Present it as reasoning from the
road network, clearly not as measurement, and keep it to a paragraph.

**Stringline aggregates** — structure verified:

- `site/data/routes/{A,E,J,Z}.json`, keyed by direction, each with `stations`
  (id, name, km), `pairs` (per block: `n`, `p50`, `p90`), and `waits`
  (per block, median minutes)
- `site/data/system.json` — 436 days, first 2021-09-01, last 2025-12-31, block
  definitions, per-route summaries
- Confirmed present: `Howard Beach-JFK Airport` (H03S) on the A;
  `Sutphin Blvd-Archer Av-JFK Airport` on the E, J and Z

**MTA bus segment speeds** — two datasets needed for continuous history, both
verified 2026-08-17:

| dataset | window | M60+ rows | Q70+ rows |
|---|---|---|---|
| `58t6-89vi` | 2023-01-01 → 2024-12-01 | 16,888 | 12,360 |
| `kufs-yh3x` | 2025-01-01 → 2026-06-01 | 12,744 | 9,119 |

Columns: `timestamp`, `day_of_week`, `hour_of_day`, `route_id`, `direction`,
`borough`, `stop_order`, `timepoint_stop_id`/`_name`/`_latitude`/`_longitude`,
the same four for `next_timepoint_stop_*`, `road_distance`,
`average_travel_time`, `average_road_speed`, `bus_trip_count`.

Note the route IDs carry a `+` suffix for Select Bus Service: **`M60+` and
`Q70+`**, not `M60`/`Q70`. Querying the unsuffixed names returns nothing.

**A units trap, checked rather than assumed.** `average_travel_time` is in
**minutes**, despite segment values in the single digits looking like seconds at
a glance. It reconciles exactly against the other two fields —
`road_distance / average_road_speed × 60` reproduces it to the decimal. Confirmed
on the Q70+ segment from 74 St/Roosevelt Av station to LaGuardia Terminal C: 3.99
miles, 14.2–15.9 minutes at 5pm, 15.0–16.8 mph. Reading it as seconds would imply
900 mph, so the error is loud if made — but assert the unit in a test anyway,
because a quieter version of this mistake is how the stringline timestamp bug
happened.

**AirTrain** is a fixed schedule rather than a measured distribution. Treat its
leg as a constant plus a headway wait, state the assumption, and do not dress it
up as observed. The same applies to the walk and transfer at Howard Beach,
Sutphin Blvd, and between subway and bus for LGA.

## Shape of the build

Separate repo, consuming stringline's published aggregates rather than importing
its code, so neither project constrains the other.

```
data_prep/
  fetch_tlc.py      duckdb+httpfs over remote parquet -> airport trips, cached
  car_times.py      distributions by origin zone and block -> site/data/car.json
  rail_times.py     stringline aggregates + AirTrain -> site/data/rail.json
  bus_times.py      segment speeds -> LGA journey times -> site/data/bus.json
  leave_by.py       the modes combined into leave-by times -> site/data/leaveby.json
site/
  index.html        one page, three questions
  app.js
  style.css
  data/*.json
```

Static page, committed JSON, no server — same as stringline.

**The artifact:** pick your neighbourhood, see your leave-by time by mode, with
the full distribution behind it rather than a single number. That is the thing
people share, and it is what makes the finding personal in the way stringline's
own commute was. Pick a real trip of your own for the walkthrough.

### Testing

- The percentile helper recovers known quantiles from synthetic histograms.
- Convolving two histograms reproduces a known combined distribution — and
  specifically demonstrates that adding p90s overstates the tail, which is the
  error the whole design exists to avoid.
- Car and rail sides are restricted to the same origins, blocks and sample floor
  before any comparison is drawn; assert this rather than trusting it.
- `average_travel_time` is asserted to be minutes, by checking it against
  `road_distance / average_road_speed × 60` on real rows. If MTA ever changes the
  unit, this fails loudly instead of silently producing a 900 mph bus.
- Spot-check one origin-hour against Todd's 2015 figures. They should differ —
  eleven years and a different vehicle mix — but a wild divergence means a bug,
  not a finding.
- Renders on a phone. Stringline needed four commits to get its chart legible on
  a small screen.

## Risks

**It reads as derivative.** Real risk, and the mitigation is to lead with the
mode question rather than the update. If the piece opens on "how long does it
take," it is Todd's post with newer data. If it opens on "should you take the
train," it is a new question that happens to need his groundwork.

**The inversion may not hold.** Rail may be both slower and looser in the tail,
in which case there is no counterintuitive punchline. Publish it anyway — a
measured refutation of universal travel-blog advice is a perfectly good piece —
but the headline has to change and the writing should not strain to rescue it.

**The AirTrain leg is assumed, not measured.** It is the weakest link in the
rail chain. Keep it explicit and separable so a reader can see what rests on it.

**The LGA bus numbers get read as stronger than they are.** This is the most
likely way the piece misleads someone, because averaged data understates bus
unpredictability and therefore favours the bus — the exact direction that would
make a wrong recommendation. Mitigations: a distinct visual encoding, the
limitation stated at every appearance rather than once in a methods note, and no
leave-by time quoted for LGA transit at a percentile the data cannot support.

**Scope creep into reconstructing the bus archive.** Tempting, since it would fix
the LGA problem properly and the data is sitting there. It is also a
subwaydata.nyc-scale job and it cannot cover the current era. Keep it as the
follow-up; do not let it swallow this piece.

## Built, and what the numbers turned out to say

All stages are implemented and committed. 82 tests pass. One month of car data
(April 2026) is loaded locally as a correctness check; the full backfill is left
to the GitHub Action, which runs the fetch on a runner rather than a laptop
because the cost is bandwidth, not processor.

**The mode question resolved differently from the guess in this spec.** The
prediction was an inversion - transit slower but tighter. What the data shows is
sharper and more geographic. Counting zones where both modes are measured, for
JFK:

| part of day | car wins | transit wins | too close |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| before 7am | 70 | 0 | 0 |
| 7–10am | 51 | 6 | 10 |
| 10am–4pm | 40 | 20 | 10 |
| 4–7pm | 26 | 26 | 8 |
| after 7pm | 65 | 0 | 0 |

So **the train only competes from Manhattan, and only in the middle of the
day**. Overnight and in the evening the car wins everywhere, which makes sense:
empty roads, thin subway service. At rush hour from Midtown the train wins
decisively - Times Square to JFK is 103 minutes by car at the ninetieth
percentile against 73 by train, and about a tenth of the fare.

An earlier note in this project generalised from the Midtown case to "the train
wins on both counts". That was too broad. The finding is conditional on where you
start and when you leave, and the page is built to make that conditionality the
subject rather than a caveat.

For LaGuardia the car wins nearly everywhere, which is the honest result even
before allowing for the bus data flattering the bus.

Because the transit side is combined conservatively, the transit column above is
a floor: the true count of transit wins is somewhat higher.

## Open: why is the measured car fare above a real quote?

**Status: not a code bug. Fares still hidden pending one live quote.**

### Settled: there is no double count

`data_prep/check_fare.py`, run 2026-08-17 against April 2026, to JFK, 4–7pm
weekdays, medians in dollars:

| zone | trips | base | tolls | taxes | airport | cbd | base+tolls | total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Garment District | 305 | 115 | 7 | 17 | 3.50 | 1.50 | 122 | 144 |
| Midtown South | 587 | 110 | 7 | 16 | 3.50 | 1.50 | 117 | 138 |
| Penn Station/Mad Sq W | 227 | 103 | 7 | 16 | 3.50 | 1.50 | 110 | 131 |

`base_passenger_fare` alone is already $103–115. Everything added on top comes to
about $28. TLC's dictionary is accurate and the fare expression is correct as
written. The earlier suspicion rested on a median of $152 being close to twice
$76 — a coincidence, not a signal, and a reminder that a tidy-looking ratio is
not evidence.

### Still open: composition, or surge, or both

A real UberX quote for the same trip and hour was **$80**, against a measured
base of $110 and an all-in median of $138. Two candidate explanations remain:

**Service tier.** These files carry UberX, Comfort, XL, Black and SUV together.
Midtown to JFK is a business route where premium tiers are common, and they
would lift the median. **This cannot be tested from the data**: the FHVHV files
have no service-tier column, so there is no way to isolate UberX. That is a hard
limit, not something more querying will fix.

**Scheduled versus hailed.** The $80 was booked the night before. Scheduled fares
are commonly priced off peak surge, and every trip in this dataset was hailed
live. A live quote at the same hour would remove this variable entirely.

The two are not exclusive and may both contribute.

### What would resolve it

A live UberX quote from Herald Square to JFK at around 5pm on a weekday, all-in.

- lands near $130 → the earlier $80 was a scheduling discount, the figures are
  sound, and `SHOW_FARES` can go on with a note that the median spans all tiers.
- lands near $80 again → tier mix is doing the work, the median is not the price
  most readers would pay, and the fare should either stay hidden or be labelled
  unambiguously as an all-tiers median rather than a quote.

Either way the fare expression stays as it is. Do not tune output to a quote.

Travel times are unaffected throughout; the fare is an aside. `SHOW_FARES` in
`site/app.js` restores it everywhere at once.

## Order of work
